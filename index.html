The keynote

Multilingual crisis communication: the human-centred pipeline for timely communication

Abstract

In this presentation, I argue that it is time to envision the design of a human-centred pipeline for multilingual crisis communication. It would enable a shift from the too many ad hoc solutions, which are still prevalent in many disasters, to creating sustainable infrastructures, which could support disaster cycle from early warning systems, through response, to recovery. More powerful machine learning solutions, such as large language models, have opened opportunities but also reignited lazy conceptualisations that standalone automated language translation is sufficient.

My argument is that the traditional disaster response cycle must evolve beyond standalone technological interventions to embrace congruous workflows where actions, activities, and technologies for multilingual communication function as an integrated system rather than discrete, separate, and standalone tools (Briva-Iglesias & O’Brien, 2026). Climate change intensifies disaster frequency annually, hence wasteful and ephemeral deployments of limited resources become extremely difficult to justify, when technological solutions, which are power hungry and impose infrastructural demands, may be perceived as contributing to, rather than resolving the problem. Given that technological resources for multilingual communication require substantial investment to produce and maintain, embedding them in sustainable pipelines that support all disaster phases is more critical than ever.

Any form of machine learning supporting multilingual communication generates resources: the problem is whether these resources are always curated, sustained, monitored, and updated. Evidence suggests that they are often produced ad hoc and not integrated. A collaborative aim for researchers working on this space would be to explore why there is not yet a start-to-end human-centred pipeline. A multilingual crisis communication pipeline may integrate all types of technology and data to ensure urgent, timely, efficient, and accurate information is available in crisis contexts. This gap represents both a technical challenge and an opportunity for interdisciplinary research communities (LT, translation studies, disaster management, behavioural psychologists) as well as a call to actively involve policymakers, NGOs, industry, public officers, and crisis and emergency managers.

While disasters may be triggered unexpectedly by latent or well-known natural or human hazards, their impact regularly disproportionately affects marginalized groups in society as it compounds over existing vulnerabilities (Alexander & Pescaroli, 2020; Federici, 2023). Community-led initiatives could integrate authentic language needs – spoken dialects, vs written languages, low-literacy vs preference for infographics - with data on language distribution from reliable censuses. Furthermore, establishing networks with culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) communities has the benefit of helping us understand which languages, channels of communication, means of access, and capacity to understand information exists (Brandon & Maang, 2022; Federici et al., 2022). Knowing communities' preferences is not always possible, but when it is, it allows us to maximise resource use so that when technologies are deployed, developed, and maintained, they create resources usable across the whole disaster cycle.

Linguistic diversity can be considered a vulnerability (Federici, 2020) in multilingual contexts where information does not reach people whose primary languages are other than the main societal language. Yet with current capacity to gather data, we must ask: why are there not more systems that leverage known language census data to build resources supporting communities with limited proficiency? In high-resource language combinations, data visualisation and evaluation tools identify language needs and their distribution (Llewellyn et al., 2026). My first reflection focuses on whether it is possible to create bespoke solutions, for instance to support early warning systems (EWS) for severe weather (Gaviria Pabón et al., 2025) that could use a standard architecture enabling integration of multiple languages. Undeniably, there are huge differences between resource-rich contexts and resource-poor contexts, where poverty includes digital poverty and a lack of sufficient low-resource data to train any NMTs or LLMs with significant accuracy to support integrated multilingual early warning systems (Reid & Samson Nkoka, 2025); once a multilingual EWS infrastructure is available, its use could support any type of warning (public health, disaster, attacks). For our LT audience from Asia specifically, this presents a critical research frontier in low-resource NLP (Todorova, 2024).

A second reflection stems from the first one. There has been extraordinary evidence of communities rallying to complement top-down, institutional dissemination of information, with the use of their own channels to communicate information in a way that makes them trusted sources (YCI, 2016). Scaling-up resources to educate people and reduce risks takes time when language resources are scarce and there is no capacity (Knauerhase et al., 2026). When needs, demands, and capacity were supported by crowdsourcing and technological expertise, resources could be and were created (Lewis, 2010; Lewis et al., 2011). Crucially, community involvement in design and deployment would ensure additional buy-in and sustainability; for the LT field, this lesson deserves to be integrated into crisis-oriented systems (Larroyed et al., 2024), but how could this be done?

My third reflection will focus discussing how we could envision such a consolidated pipeline. Would it be a system, an architecture, or a process? Whichever form it would take, the high-stakes communicative setting demand a human-centred approach (Briva-Iglesias, 2024; Briva-Iglesias & O’Brien, 2026; Carpuat et al., 2025; O'Brien, 2024). With such a system or human-centred pipeline, validated and trustworthy resources can be re-used and optimised to generate new resources automatically, with a higher degree of quality in output.

This vision for an integrated pipeline is possibly utopian at this stage. Many questions and objections can be raised: who owns it, where does it sit, who pays for its creation and maintenance, how does it work in contexts of war and conflict? They are all valid especially at a time of extreme diplomatic and political instability globally. The answers could be bottom-up: local authorities could focus on local needs and network with local communities; creating bottom-up networks in the 21st century is extremely easy. If there is a political willingness to create resources to support multilingual communication in crises, the priority will be to test the feasibility of bottom-up systems that validate and share resources for multilingual communities, rather than duplicating work and depleting small budgets with redundant translations, Then, locally viable solutions can be easily networked. If international instability is a barrier, there are also established actors who will could harness such re-usable resources to deploy them in isolated, poor, conflict-torn, and disaster-affected regions. Many organisations such as the Emergency Medical Teams and Médecins sans Frontières have embedded some degree of training for their translation and interpreting teams; giving them access to optimised technological pipelines would not be seen as difficult. Also, organisation such as Crisis Translation Response and Translators without Borders (CLEAR Global) could integrate this hypothetical pipeline in their existing approaches. As scaling-up resources is possible in commercial settings, reusing resources in disaster settings should be more than a moral code, but an infrastructural and design challenge that requires technological solutions and drives brilliant minds to devise a new architecture. Professional translators and interpreters will then be paid properly from budgets that have demonstrated how cost-effective does not mean expensive ad hoc solutions, but long-term optimisation of resources. For pivot and mid-resourced languages, such a system can gradually create additional resources that are domain-specific and for low-resource languages, the pipeline can support training of professional translators.

These reflections could be dismissed as unachievable and idealistic. Perhaps they are. However, they also provide an overview of ideas coalescing around the findings from studies that show quantitatively that how the impact of high-quality translation (Ray et al., 2025), interpreting, and multimodal communication can be measured (Hibbs et al., 2026). If we can measure how risks (especially health-related risks) can be communicated in multilingual settings, then we will have parameters to evaluate the outputs of any integrated system. It will be possible to scale up evaluations on whether human-centred and optimised pipelines can produce trustworthy translations of risks, whose impact can be measured, and negative outcomes reduced.

About the speaker

Professor Federico M. Federici is Director of the Centre for Translation Studies at the University College and a Professor of Intercultural Crisis Communication at the university. Dr. Federici has published extensively on language translation in crises, with his current research centred on multilingual communication in crises, in particular, translated communication in medical and healthcare contexts. He was a Co-Investigator on the INTERACT project, a large multi-year EU-funded project, partnering with colleagues at Dublin City University and other European institutions, with a research focus on crisis translation. Following a project funded by the British Academy, investigating COVID-19 vaccination hesitancy among migrants in Italy and the role of translators, mediators, and interpreters in disseminating health-related information Dr. Federici’s work extended towards multilingual healthcare. His projects now go beyond crises, looking at the use of translation in doctor-patient interactions, in particular, in scenarios where patients have limited English proficiency, and the impact that translation (both human-mediated and automated) has on patient care and outcomes

Call for Papers

The language technologies (LTs) that were applied in response to the Haitian earthquake of 2010 showed to the world the life-saving potential for these technologies, where their application was responsible for saving 1,000s of lives. Most concerning is that in a world that is hit by more than 350 disasters in an average year, the most severe of these disasters occur in regions where English is not spoken. With the development of massively popular AI systems used as general-purpose machines, the potential of LTs in disaster preparedness and response has rapidly grown. In response, the focus of this workshop is on the direct application of LTs in crisis preparedness and response, including recent work on multilingual and multimodal LT.

We invite submissions to the First Workshop on Language Technologies for Crisis Preparedness and Response (LT4CPR) at AACL 2026. The workshop focuses on advancing speech and language technologies that support crisis preparedness, crisis response, communication in crises and disaster risk reduction, and aims to bring together LT research communities with crisis management stakeholders (such as practitioners, NGOs, and industry) to bring to the fore interdisciplinary solutions that have real-world impact.

In this first edition, we especially welcome work on:

  • Identifying Community Needs: The crisis ecosystem is quite diverse, composed by affected populations, policy-makers, NGOs, industry, public officers, and emergency management.
  • Interdisciplinary approaches for analysis, design, and evaluation of LT systems: insights from communication studies, social sciences, psychology, among others, on how to design and evaluate trustworthy crisis technology that fits stakeholders needs.
  • Translating LT research into operational tools: how to go beyond AI model development to user adoption of technology in high-stakes scenarios.
  • Ethics and societal implications of AI in crisis communication and management.

Topics of Interest

We invite submissions that fall under the following list (but not limited to):

  • Research work on AI for crisis response, such as information extraction (e.g., NER and geolocation), situational awareness (e.g., automated triage), and more
  • Qualitative studies to understand user needs and guide technology design
  • Multilingual and multimodal research on crisis and humanitarian initiatives
  • Evaluation and deployment considerations
  • Automatic approaches to enhance or facilitate communication from and to communities during crisis (e.g., computational social media and news analysis, summarization, translation, etc.)
  • Position papers and literature surveys on current trends, challenges and future directions
  • Abstracts for talk proposals or field reports from practitioners, NGOs, humanitarian organizations, and field experts, including experience reports

Submission Types

The workshop will accept two types of papers:

  1. Archival submissions: Papers of up to 8 pages of content, plus unlimited pages for limitations, references and appendices. Accepted papers will be given an additional page of content to address reviewer comments. Previously published papers cannot be accepted.
  2. Non-Archival submissions: Up to 8 pages, excluding references, to summarise and showcase in-progress work and work published elsewhere. These papers may be presented at the workshop but will not appear in the proceedings.

Presentation Mode

We invite researchers, students, practitioners, humanitarian organizations, and industry teams to participate in the LT4CPR Shared Task on Automatic Situation Report (Sitrep) Generation, organized as part of the First Workshop on Language Technologies for Crisis Preparedness and Response (LT4CPR) at AACL-IJCNLP 2026, on November 9, in Hengqin, Zhuhai, China.

During a crisis, responders must rapidly synthesize large volumes of fragmented, repetitive, uncertain, and sometimes conflicting information. Social media can provide timely reports about hazards, impacts, infrastructure damage, affected populations, urgent needs, and response activities, but turning these messages into an operationally useful overview remains labor-intensive. This shared task explores how language technologies can support the generation of concise, structured, and verifiable situation reports from crisis-related social media streams.

Task Overview

Participating systems will receive a collection of crisis-related social media messages and generate a situation report that summarizes the most important information contained in the collection.

  • Input: a set of crisis-related social media messages.
  • Output: a structured situation report following the official task template and output format.
  • Goal: produce a report that is informative, concise, well organized, faithful to the source messages, and useful for situational awareness.

Reports may cover information such as the crisis context, major events, affected people and locations, casualties, displacement, infrastructure and service disruptions, emerging needs, response activities, resource provision, and unresolved or uncertain claims. The final schema and required sections will be specified in the task guidelines.

No prior participation in LT4CPR is required, and interdisciplinary teams are especially encouraged.

How to Participate

System Description Papers

Participating teams will be invited to submit a system description paper explaining their approach, resources, experiments, results, error analysis, limitations, and ethical considerations. Papers should clearly disclose the models, external data, APIs, retrieval sources, and manual interventions used by the system.

  • Submission format: official ACL style; up to 8 pages of content, plus unlimited pages for limitations, references, and appendices.
  • Accepted papers: accepted papers will be given one additional page of content to address reviewer comments.
  • Prior publication: previously published papers cannot be accepted.
  • Submission deadline: September 15, 2026, 11:59 p.m. AoE.
  • Submission portal: system paper submission instructions and OpenReview link.
  • Presentation: accepted system papers may be presented as posters or sessions at the LT4CPR workshop.
  • Proceedings: publication details will be announced with the system-paper call.

Participation Rules

  • Each team must designate a primary contact and use a consistent team name across registration and submissions.
  • Participants must comply with the dataset license and may not redistribute restricted task data.
  • All external data, pretrained models, APIs, prompts, and human involvement must be documented.
  • Submitted outputs must be produced by the registered system by the submission deadline.
  • Teams must follow the submission limits and resource restrictions of their selected track.
  • The organizers reserve the right to inspect submissions for rule compliance and to exclude invalid entries.

Ethics and Responsible Use

Crisis-related social media can contain sensitive personal information, distressing content, rumors, and references to vulnerable populations. Participants must use the data only for the purposes allowed by the dataset license and should avoid attempts to identify, contact, or profile individuals represented in the data. Teams should discuss privacy, representational harms, hallucination risks, model limitations, and the risks of deploying automated reports in high-stakes environments.

Shared-task outputs are research artifacts and must not be treated as verified operational intelligence or used as a substitute for expert review and established emergency-management procedures.

Camera-Ready Q&A

When are camera-ready submissions due?

The deadline for research and shared-task papers has been extended from October 10 to Monday, October 12, 2026 (23:59 AoE).

How should I format the final version?

Submit the final, non-anonymous version with all author names and affiliations restored and line numbers removed. Use the unmodified official ACL style files in final mode; if your template uses \aclfinalcopy, uncomment it. The paper must use A4 paper in two columns, with all fonts embedded and no page numbers.

What is the camera-ready page limit?

We suggest 8 pages of main text for camera-ready papers. Accepted archival papers may include up to one additional page to address reviewer comments, for a maximum of 9 pages of main text, plus unlimited pages for limitations, references and appendices, consistent with the workshop's published page limits. Non-archival papers may use up to 8 pages, excluding references.

Which files should I submit?

Submit both the final PDF and the corresponding LaTeX or Word source.

What should I check before submitting?

Run a quick check with ACL pubcheck before sending your files, and resolve any formatting issues it identifies. Confirm that author names and affiliations are present, fonts are embedded, and line numbers and page numbers are absent.

Registration and Presentation Q&A

Updated: October 10, 2026

Is registration required for both virtual and in-person presentations?

Yes. All papers presented at the workshop must be registered, whether the presentation is virtual or in person.

Can a shared-task paper appear in the proceedings without registration or a presentation?

Yes. Shared-task papers do not require registration to appear in the proceedings. This exception is now confirmed and allows shared-task papers to be included without a presentation. Registration is still required for papers presented at the workshop, whether virtually or in person.
